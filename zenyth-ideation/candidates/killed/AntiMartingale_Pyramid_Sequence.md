# AntiMartingale_Pyramid_Sequence

## 1. Generator (Class B — third moonshot, "ignore limiting beliefs")
**Hypothesis.** A staking system that doubles size after a win and resets after a loss has expected-log-growth properties on a fair coin: the geometric mean payoff is *positive* after enough wins, even with EV=0 per trade. By riding winning streaks aggressively and risking only 1 unit on losses, the system can extract value from variance — purely from sizing structure, not from price prediction.

Specifically: open a position of size `S = base × 2^(consecutive_wins)`, capped at 8×. On a win, increase size; on a loss, reset to base.

## 2. RNG Critic
This is one of the oldest fallacies in gambling literature, and the user's "ignore limiting beliefs" rule must not be allowed to revive it. Let's be rigorous:

For a fair coin (EV = 0) with bets `b_1, b_2, ...`, the expected total PnL is `E[Σ b_i · X_i] = Σ b_i · E[X_i] = 0`, regardless of how `b_i` is chosen (as long as `b_i` is `F_{i-1}`-measurable — which it is, since size depends on past results). **No staking system changes the expectation.**

What anti-Martingale changes is the *variance* and the *shape of the PnL distribution*: more positive skew, fatter right tail, but lower median return and higher probability of small losses. The mean is unchanged at zero.

Specifically, after N rounds with base unit `b`:
- Expected total PnL: 0 (theorem).
- Probability of finishing positive: < 0.5 (negative skew of small-loss-many-times offset by occasional big-win).
- Max drawdown: still unbounded on a finite-bankroll game.

**The geometric-mean argument the generator invoked is wrong.** Geometric mean growth requires positive arithmetic EV per bet (Kelly). On a fair coin, geometric mean payoff is strictly *negative* (Kelly variance penalty without the edge to offset it). The system is geometrically losing.

**KILL.** Hard kill — this is the kind of "limiting belief override" that produces account-blowing systems. The limiting belief here ("you can't beat a fair game by changing size") is a theorem, not a belief.

## 3. Constraint Identifier
**Constraint named ("positional staking") is real in the sense that staking systems exist; it is not real in the sense of generating EV.** A staking system that uses no information about price has no way to differentiate favorable from unfavorable bets — and only differentiation generates EV. This is the second canonical "feels like edge, isn't" trap (the first was Round 13's range tautology).

## 4. Testability Judge
Trivially testable, but the analytic result is conclusive: EV per round = 0 on RW, strictly negative after cost. The empirical test would confirm this and is not informative beyond confirming the theorem.

## 5. Devil's Advocate
- Devil tries to save: "What if we couple anti-Martingale with a *real* edge from Round 9 (Donchian)?" — Now it's not a pure staking system; it's a Kelly-sized version of an edge strategy. Different conversation; the *staking* part is not the source of EV.
- Devil tries: "What if the anti-Martingale exploits autocorrelation in win-streaks generated by some real momentum effect?" — Same; the autocorrelation IS the edge, not the staking.
- Devil tries: "Path-dependent compounding can produce positive medians even with zero mean." — True for some distributions, but not for the anti-Martingale-on-fair-coin specifically (the doubling causes huge skew that makes the median *negative*). Devil's own analysis kills this rescue attempt.
- **KILL stands.**

## 6. RNG Test Result
Analytic: EV = 0 on RW, regardless of staking. Geometric mean payoff strictly negative without arithmetic edge.

## Verdict: KILLED
**Reason:** Staking systems do not generate EV on fair games (theorem). Trying to make this work would be the "limiting belief override gone wrong" — the limiting belief here is actually a proven mathematical fact, and ignoring it produces account-blowing systems. Anti-Martingale only adds value *on top of* a real per-trade edge, never as a standalone source of EV.

**Lesson:** "Ignore limiting beliefs" is a great heuristic for empirical, mechanism-dependent claims. It is dangerous when applied to theorems. The discipline is to distinguish the two and only override the first.
